The new lab notebooks on this site begin with squared paper. There is a fold through the spread, a colour that follows the notebook from its collection into the pages, and space for a small diagram beside the writing. I wanted them to feel like working objects worth opening: the notebooks that power things.

The squares were an early part of that feeling. They gave the pages a common surface, something a drawing and a paragraph could both belong to. But the more useful change was in the relationship between those things. A description of an experiment could have its apparatus beside it. A result could keep its conditions close. The next page could answer the question raised by the previous one.

[The repair notebook](https://chrishayuk.com/notebook/the-repairer-left-the-mechanism-kept-working#experiment) gives a concrete example. Its agents work with a record of four numbers. A small diagram helps establish that world before the reader encounters the comparison between written instructions and an executable repair mechanism. The drawing gives the words somewhere to land. Later, the result keeps the individual worlds visible rather than reducing the experiment to a single impressive number.

That is the kind of page I want a lab notebook to hold. The question, the setup, what happened and the limits should be reachable from one another. A diagram can introduce a mechanism; a table can retain the detail; an annotation can keep a qualification beside the claim it changes. They have different jobs within the same spread.

The image behind this is partly a codex. Leonardo’s notebooks are a useful reference for the proximity of writing and drawing. The V&A describes geometry and hydraulics alongside architecture, clothing and anatomy in the Codex Forster III. I am interested in that shared space for different kinds of investigation. It is a compositional reference for these pages. [V&A: Codex Forster III](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/explore-leonardo-da-vincis-notebooks-codex-forster-iii).

The historical object also needs some care. The British Library describes the Codex Arundel as a collection of papers, including material gathered together after Leonardo’s death. The arrangement we encounter has a history of its own. My notebook pages are likewise a deliberate presentation of working material, although the work and the circumstances are entirely different. [British Library: Codex Arundel](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002039546).

The lab notebook is still an exhibition. The page directs attention through placement, scale and sequence. A large question can open the spread. The apparatus can sit beside the explanation. A result can arrive after the reader knows what was measured. A quiet margin can hold the reason to be cautious. The aim is to make close reading feel inviting.

That matters in [the notebook about task permission](https://chrishayuk.com/notebook/the-page-could-ask-it-couldnt-authorise#n-machine-task-notebook-folio-3). The page places the site’s invitation, the assigned task and the recorded outcome together. The diagram represents supplied information and observed behaviour. It does not reveal an agent’s internal reasoning. Its caption belongs beside the drawing because the distinction is part of what the drawing means.

The paper colour should carry through that whole experience. Choosing a notebook from a collection should lead into the same object. Diagrams, chart labels and controls need to feel written or mounted on its pages. During the redesign, some instruments still carried dark panels from the earlier exhibition treatment. That made them feel pasted in from another design. Bringing their lines, labels and surfaces into the notebook palette is part of making the page coherent.

Film and recorded worlds need a different kind of care. Their images have information of their own. A film can have a mount and a caption without recolouring its pixels. A recorded simulation can retain its display while the surrounding controls, labels and population chart belong to the paper. In [the Cell80 mutation notebook](https://chrishayuk.com/notebook/can-you-name-the-mutation-that-changed-a-world#open-notebook), the reader can replay recorded states and inspect the intervention. The book becomes something to operate as well as read.

The physical suggestion should also make the digital object easier to use. Page controls belong on the notebook, where the reader can reach them. Content needs room to grow. A long figure should remain readable; fitting it into an attractive rectangle is a poor reason to cut it off. The same spread needs to rearrange itself when the available space becomes narrow.

Each lab notebook also needs an ending. A conclusion gives the reader somewhere to gather what the experiment established, what remains uncertain and what might follow. It can be provisional. A notebook with an unresolved result still has something to say about where the work has reached. The publication history and underlying sources keep that interpretation connected to its record.

This does not make every notebook a lab notebook. The [exhibition-design note](https://chrishayuk.com/notebook/i-wanted-a-website-to-behave-like-an-exhibition#open-notebook) follows references and visual decisions; it needs the freedom of a lookbook. Other notes develop a theme or hold an early proposition. They can belong to the same family while giving their material a different rhythm. The lab style is one member of that family.

For HAUSE, the useful design problem is to hold those differences together. Typography, page turning and publication details can be shared. The composition must still respond to the material. A measured comparison, a film, a visual reference and a speculative question should each be recognisable as the thing it is.

There is a responsibility in making working material look finished. A composed page can lend confidence to an uncertain claim. The notebook has to keep its working distinctions visible: recorded and proposed, measured and illustrated, published and still in draft. The exhibition succeeds when it helps the reader notice those distinctions and inspect the work more closely.

That is what the new lab-notebook style is trying to do. It gives the experiments a place that feels considered, tangible and open to examination. The squares, colours and drawings invite someone into the work. The evidence, conclusions and unanswered questions give them a reason to stay.
